Grid Operators Warn Summer Reserve Margins Are Thinner Than Forecast

Three regional operators told federal regulators this week that projected August reserves fall below their own planning targets, citing delayed transmission upgrades and a hotter overnight load.

By Adaeze Okonjo and Martin Reyes
Published July 30, 2026 at 6:12 AM. 9 min read. Updated July 30 at 9:14 AM

Read at Meridian Wire

Operators responsible for keeping the lights on across roughly a third of the country told the Federal Energy Regulatory Commission on Tuesday that their August reserve margins now sit below the levels their own planning documents call adequate, a shift they attributed less to a shortage of generation than to equipment that has not been installed on schedule.

The filings, submitted separately by three regional transmission organizations, describe a summer in which demand has held above forecast overnight rather than only at the late afternoon peak. That pattern, operators wrote, compresses the window they normally use to bring units back from maintenance.

"We are not short of megawatts on paper," said Denise Aturu, who directs system planning at one of the three organizations. "We are short of the hours we usually count on to get equipment back in service, and that is a different problem with a different fix."

What changed since the spring assessment

In April, the same organizations projected reserve margins between 17 and 21 percent for the peak week of August. The figures filed this week fall between 12 and 16 percent. Two of the three now sit under their internal target, which is not the same as a reliability violation but does trigger a more conservative posture toward scheduled outages.

Three factors account for most of the revision, according to the filings:

  • Transformer and breaker replacements at eleven substations slipped past their spring commissioning dates, most of them waiting on components with lead times that have not recovered since 2023.
  • Overnight minimum load ran 4 to 7 percent above the April forecast in June and the first three weeks of July, driven by data center ramps and by residential cooling that no longer drops off after midnight.
  • Two combined cycle plants that were expected to return from extended outages in June are now scheduled for late August.

The margin math looks the same whether a plant is missing or a transformer is missing. What is different is who fixes it and how long they have.

None of the three operators asked the commission for emergency relief. All three asked for expedited treatment of pending interconnection agreements, which would not change August at all but would affect the 2027 and 2028 planning years.

"We are short of the hours we usually count on to get equipment back in service."

The overnight load is the quieter story

Utility planners have spent two decades treating the summer afternoon as the moment that defines system adequacy. The filings suggest that framing is aging. When overnight minimums rise, the system loses the slack it uses for maintenance, and the cost shows up months later as deferred work rather than as a visible shortfall.

Commission staff have not set a date for a technical conference. A spokesperson said the filings are under review and noted that the commission's own summer assessment, published in May, flagged transmission equipment delivery as a watch item in two of the three regions.

How reserve margin is measured

Reserve margin compares expected available capacity against forecast peak demand, expressed as a percentage above that peak. A 15 percent margin means the operator expects 15 percent more capacity than it expects to need at the peak hour. Targets vary by region and are set by the operator, not by the commission.

State regulators in two of the affected states have scheduled their own hearings for mid-August. Utilities in both states have said publicly that they do not anticipate rotating outages, and both filings this week say the same, with the caveat that a multi-day heat event across two regions at once remains the scenario that stresses the assumptions hardest.

For readers outside the three regions, the practical signal is narrower than the headline number suggests. Reserve margin is a planning statistic, not a forecast of outages, and it moves for reasons that have nothing to do with whether a given August afternoon goes smoothly. What the filings document is a supply chain problem arriving on a planner's calendar.

Aturu put it more plainly. "Ask me in October," she said. "If we spend the fall catching up on the maintenance we deferred this summer, then this was a hard year. If we do not, then this is the first year of a pattern."
